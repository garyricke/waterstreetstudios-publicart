01 / THE IDEA

Walk the town.
Press record.
Ask five questions.

The whole proposal in one sentence

Gary spends a morning or an afternoon with one person — or a small group — and we physically go to every piece of public art in Batavia. At each stop, they tell the story: how it came to be, who the artist was, what nearly derailed it, what nobody notices. It is a conversation, not a shoot. No script, no teleprompter, no crew.

At every stop we also grab 30–60 seconds of Insta360 footage and a short drone move. That is the visual seasoning that keeps fifty reels from looking like fifty talking heads.

Then the work stops being human. Each interview gets broken down into one or several short reels, each with the same branded end card and call to action, and a scheduling agent releases them one per week — forever.

The strategic point

Batavia already knows it has good public art. Very few people know who keeps making it happen. Fifty-two weeks of stories, every one of them signed the same way, fixes that.

This is a branding program disguised as a content program. The reels are genuinely about the art and the artists — that is why people watch them — but the cumulative effect is that Water Street Studios becomes the answer to "where does Batavia's public art come from?"

02 / THE MATH

Capture once.
Cut many.
Publish forever.

The reason weekly video dies at small organizations is that every post is treated as its own project. Someone has an idea, someone films it, someone edits it, someone remembers to post it. That is roughly two to three hours per post, and it collapses the first busy month.

We invert it. Filming happens in rare, dense batches. Editing is a program. Publishing is a cron job. Human judgment is spent once a month on approvals, not once a week on production.

05 / CAPTURE DAYS

Two mornings
fill the whole year.

Capture Day 01 — Opportunistic

America 250 Unveiling

Saturday, September 12, 2026 · Batavia City Hall

Gary is at a wedding that day but can swing through. This is a constrained, high-value window — the artist, the committee, the officials, and a finished mural are all in one place at one time, and that will not happen again. The plan is built for 45–60 minutes, solo, no setup.

First 5 minutes

Drone up before the crowd builds. Two moves: a slow push toward the west bays, and one reveal pulling back over Depot Pond.

Next 10 minutes

Insta360 walk-through of the finished mural, plus tight detail passes on two or three panels. No people needed.

30–40 minutes

Four interviews, roughly eight minutes each, standing in front of the work. Priority order below. Same five questions for everyone.

Last 5 minutes

Ambient crowd audio, reaction shots, one wide of people looking up at it. Then leave for the wedding.

Interview priority — in this order

  1. 01The selected artist — irreplaceable. If only one interview happens, this is it.
  2. 02A committee member — the "how this got funded and approved" story.
  3. 03A City of Batavia representative — the civic frame; useful for grant reporting too.
  4. 04Two members of the public — 20 seconds each. The most shareable footage of the day.
Someone needs to warn the artist and the committee in advance that we will ask for eight minutes. Cold-asking on unveiling day gets a rushed, useless answer.

A Saturday morning — late Sept or October 2026 · ~4 hours

This is the one that fills the calendar. We walk downtown Batavia with one to three people who were in the room when these pieces happened, and we stop at every installation. Roughly 25–30 minutes per stop, then walk to the next one. Walking between stops is not lost time — it is where the good, unguarded stories actually come out.

The five questions

Ask everyone
the same thing.

A fixed question set is the single highest-leverage decision in this whole system. It means every interview produces the same shaped answers, which means the automation always knows what it is looking at, which means editing becomes pattern-matching instead of judgment.

It also produces natural comparison reels — six artists answering question five, cut together, is a reel we get for free.

Q1

Say your name, and tell me what you had to do with this piece.

Gives every clip its own built-in lower third. No separate logging needed.

Q2

What was here before?

The before/after reel writes itself. Boarded windows, blank brick, a parking wall.

Q3

Tell me about one thing that almost went wrong.

The highest-performing question. Conflict is what makes a story a story.

Q4

What is the detail nobody notices?

Produces a "look closer" reel per artwork and gives the drone something to point at.

Q5

Ten years from now, someone walks past this. What do you hope they feel?

The closer. Almost always the emotional beat that a reel should end on before the end card — and the answer that makes the case for public art funding better than any grant application.

Gear, kept deliberately small

Phone or mirrorless on a small gimbal, one wireless lav per speaker, Insta360, drone. Nothing that needs a second person or a setup window.

Audio is the only thing that can't be fixed later

Lav on the speaker, always. A great story with wind noise is unusable; a mediocre story with clean audio is publishable.

Releases signed on the spot

One-page media release, phone signature, before the camera comes out. This is the step that quietly kills projects six months later.

The habit we need to build

A shared upload link, and permission to send bad footage.

The failure mode is people self-censoring — deciding their vertical phone clip is "not good enough to send." It is. Shaky, 15-second, poorly-lit footage of someone painting is exactly what a b-roll insert needs, and nobody outside the committee will ever see the parts we do not use.

What "sending it" looks like

  1. 01Tap the bookmarked upload link on your phone.
  2. 02Pick the clips. Type one line: where and who.
  3. 03That is the entire ask. No editing, no naming, no thinking about whether it is usable.

For commissioned artists, we make it contractual and easy: three selfie clips during the project — one at the start, one mid-build, one on completion — written into the agreement as a deliverable alongside the artwork itself. It costs them six minutes and it doubles the storytelling value of the commission.

07 / FORMATS

Eight templates.
Endless variations.

One raw interview yields several different reels, because the same footage can be cut eight ways. The automation picks the template that matches what it finds in the transcript — a "what almost went wrong" answer becomes a Behind the Build; a one-liner becomes a One Question.

45–60s

The Story Behind

One artwork, one storyteller, one complete story. The flagship format.

15–25s

One Question

A single punchy answer, no setup. Cheapest to produce, highest volume.

20–30s

Then / Now

Boarded window to finished mural. Archive photo, hard cut, drone reveal.

30–45s

Behind the Build

The thing that almost went wrong. Process footage over the telling.

30–40s

Meet the Artist

Who they are, how they work, what else they have made. Artists reshare these.

15–20s

Look Closer

Pure visual. 360 orbit or drone into one hidden detail. No talking at all.

20–30s

Where To Find It

Walking route from one piece to the next. Drives the self-guided tour CTA.

20–30s

Six Answers

The same question, six different artists, cut together. Free from the fixed question set.

Templates also solve the hardest problem in weekly content, which is not production — it is deciding what to make. With eight formats and a tagged clip library, the question "what goes out next Wednesday?" has a mechanical answer.

We connect to the existing Water Street Studios accounts — we are not starting new ones. The public art initiative should reinforce the Water Street brand, not fragment it into a second audience we then have to grow from zero.

How we actually connect

There are two routes, and I recommend starting with the boring one.

Phase 1 — recommended start

A scheduling service the agent writes into

The automation drops approved reels and captions into a scheduler (Buffer, Metricool, or similar) that already holds the platform connections. It handles token refreshes, platform API changes, and retries — the three things that silently break homegrown publishers. If it fails, a human can publish from a phone in 30 seconds.

Phase 2 — once it is proven

Direct platform APIs

Meta Graph API for Instagram and Facebook, YouTube Data API for Shorts. Removes the subscription and gives us full control of metadata, but adds maintenance. Only worth doing after the pipeline has run reliably for a couple of months.

What this requires from Water Street

Instagram must be a Business or Creator account linked to the Facebook Page, and someone with admin rights has to grant access. This is usually the step that takes three weeks longer than expected, which is why it is on the decisions list.

09 / THE STACK

Boring tools,
wired together
on purpose.

Nothing here is exotic. The novelty is not in any single tool — it is that Claude Code sits in the middle and does the coordinating work that would otherwise be a person clicking between five apps every week.

Claude Code

The orchestrator

Reads transcripts, chooses clips, writes captions and alt text, drives every other tool, and files the output. This is the piece that replaces the weekly human.

Descript

Transcription & story edit

Turns speech into an editable text document. Removes filler words, generates captions, cuts to the transcript. Its API and agent can be driven directly from Claude Code.

ffmpeg

The deterministic finisher

Free, scriptable, exact. Handles 9:16 reframing, loudness normalization, title and end card concatenation, and export presets. The parts that must be identical every single time.

Google Drive

Ingest & archive

Six numbered folders, one upload link anyone on the committee can use from a phone. Also becomes the initiative's permanent media archive, which we currently do not have.

Scheduled agent

The weekly heartbeat

Runs on a cron schedule with no one watching. Pulls the next approved reel, publishes it, logs the result, checks queue depth, and emails if anything is off.

Review & metrics page

On this site

A password-gated page in the same pattern as the jury review page — watch the queued reels, approve them, and see what last month's posts did. No new logins for anyone.

A note on what stays human

The automation never writes a story it was not told, never generates footage, and never posts something a person has not watched. It selects, trims, formats, schedules, and reports. Everything the audience hears came out of a real person's mouth in front of a real wall in Batavia — which is precisely why the reels will be worth watching.

10 / GUARDRAILS — HOW THIS SURVIVES A BAD MONTH

The 8-week floor

If the approved queue drops below eight reels, the system flags it at the monthly check-in. We never discover we are out of content the week we run out.

Silence over filler

An empty queue means no post and an alert. The publisher has no ability to improvise, so it can never put something unvetted onto a Water Street channel.

Releases first

No footage of a person enters the pipeline without a signed one-page release on file. Artist agreements get a media clause going forward.

Manual override, always

Any committee member can pause the queue or pull a scheduled post. The automation is a convenience, never an authority.

Transcription/editing subscription, a scheduling service, and cloud storage. Order of magnitude is roughly $50–80 per month total — I will bring exact figures once we confirm which scheduler. No hardware purchase needed; the camera, Insta360, and drone are already in hand.

What this costs the committee

Ten minutes a month, one Saturday morning per half-year, and a willingness to send bad phone footage.

What it costs Gary

Two capture days a year and roughly two weeks of build time up front, most of it in September.

What it buys

Fifty-two reminders a year that Batavia's public art has a name behind it — and a permanent oral history of every piece, recorded while the people who made it are still here to tell it.

THE POINT

Right now, the story of how Batavia got its public art is held by a handful of people and written down almost nowhere. This plan records it while it is still first-hand, turns it into something people actually want to watch, and makes sure that every time they watch it, they learn who made it happen.
